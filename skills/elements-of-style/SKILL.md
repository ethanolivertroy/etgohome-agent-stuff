# The Elements of Style

> Based on *The Elements of Style* by **William Strunk Jr.** (1920 edition, public domain).

Strunk's rules for plain English, adapted for an agent that edits and writes. The core idea:
make every word tell. Apply the rules to serve the reader, not as a checklist to satisfy.

## How to use this skill

**Editing a draft:**

1. Read the whole piece first. Find its purpose and the one thing the reader must take away.
2. Fix structure before sentences: one topic per paragraph (rule 8), topic sentence first (rule 9).
3. Then go sentence by sentence through rules 10–18, then punctuation (rules 1–7).
4. Check word choice against [references/misused-words.md](references/misused-words.md).
5. Keep the author's voice, meaning, and technical terms. Do not add claims or change facts.
6. Unless asked for a clean rewrite only, return the edited text plus a short list of the most
   important changes, citing rule numbers (e.g. "cut 'the fact that' (13)").

**Writing from scratch:** apply the same rules as you draft, then do one editing pass.

When a rule and clarity conflict, choose clarity. Strunk himself says the best writers sometimes
break the rules, but only when the sentence gains something by it.

## Elementary rules of usage

1. **Possessive singular: add 's**, whatever the final consonant: *Charles's*, *Burns's*. The
   pronouns *hers, its, theirs, yours, oneself* take no apostrophe.
2. **Serial comma.** In a series of three or more, put a comma after each term but the last:
   *red, white, and blue*.
3. **Enclose parenthetic expressions between two commas**, never one. *My brother, you will be
   pleased to hear, is well.* Non-restrictive clauses (*which*) take commas; restrictive ones
   (*that*, or *who* that identifies) do not.
4. **Comma before a conjunction joining independent clauses**: *The situation is perilous, but
   there is still one chance.* Don't overuse this loose two-clause pattern (rule 14).
5. **Don't join independent clauses with a comma.** Use a semicolon, a period, or a comma plus a
   conjunction. A semicolon is still needed before *accordingly, besides, then, therefore, thus*.
6. **Don't break sentences in two.** *He was an interesting talker. A man who had traveled.* →
   join with a comma. A fragment is fine only for deliberate emphasis.
7. **An opening participial phrase must refer to the grammatical subject.** *Being in a
   dilapidated condition, I bought the house cheap* says the writer was dilapidated.

## Elementary principles of composition

8. **One paragraph per topic.** A new paragraph signals a new step. Avoid one-sentence
   paragraphs except for transitions (or in reference docs and UI copy).
9. **Begin each paragraph with a topic sentence; end in keeping with it.** Don't end on a
   digression or minor detail.
10. **Use the active voice.** *Dead leaves covered the ground*, not *There were a great number of
    dead leaves lying on the ground*. Use passive when the receiver of the action is the topic,
    and avoid noun-heavy passives: *The region was surveyed*, not *A survey of the region was made*.
11. **Put statements in positive form.** *He usually came late*, not *He was not very often on
    time*. Prefer *forgot* to *did not remember*, *ignored* to *did not pay attention to*. Use
    *not* for denial or contrast, never to hedge.
12. **Use definite, specific, concrete language.** *It rained every day for a week*, not *A period
    of unfavorable weather set in*. In technical writing: name the command, file, number, or error.
13. **Omit needless words.** This doesn't mean short sentences; it means every word earns its place.
    | Wordy | Concise |
    | --- | --- |
    | the question as to whether | whether |
    | there is no doubt but that | doubtless |
    | he is a man who | he |
    | in a hasty manner | hastily |
    | owing to the fact that | since, because |
    | in spite of the fact that | though |
    | the fact that he had not succeeded | his failure |
    | His brother, who is a member of the firm | His brother, a member of the firm |

    Revise *the fact that* out of every sentence. Combine a chain of short sentences that build
    one idea into one sentence.
14. **Avoid a succession of loose sentences.** A run of "X, and Y" / "X, but Y" sentences turns
    sing-song. Recast some as simple sentences, semicolon pairs, or periodic sentences.
15. **Express co-ordinate ideas in similar form** (parallel construction). Repeat an article or
    preposition before every term in a series or only before the first. Correlatives (*both…and,
    not…but, either…or, first…second*) take the same grammatical form: *not for words, but for
    action*. Bullet lists especially should be parallel.
16. **Keep related words together.** Don't split subject and verb with a movable phrase. Put a
    relative pronoun right after its antecedent and a modifier next to what it modifies: *He found
    only two mistakes*, not *He only found two mistakes*.
17. **In summaries, keep to one tense.** Prefer present for summarizing works. Say once that what
    follows is a summary; don't keep repeating "he said", "the author then states".
18. **Put the emphatic words at the end.** The end of a sentence (and of a paragraph, and of a
    piece) is the position of emphasis; the start is the second strongest.

## Matters of form (brief)

- **Numerals:** write dates and serial numbers as figures: *August 9, 1918*, *Rule 3*.
- **Parentheses:** punctuate the sentence as if the parenthetical were absent.
- **Quotations:** colon before a formal quotation; comma before one that is the object of a verb;
  no quotation marks after *that* (indirect discourse) or for proverbs.
- **Titles of works:** italics, capitalized.

## Outdated rules: do not apply

These were 1920 conventions. Modern usage, style guides, and readers reject them; don't "correct"
text toward them:

- **"Use *he* for *everyone/anyone/someone*."** Use singular *they*, or rephrase.
- **Never split an infinitive.** Split it when that reads more naturally (*to boldly go*).
- **Shall/will and should/would by person.** Use *will* and *would* as modern English does.
- **Hyphenate *to-day, to-night, to-morrow*.** Write *today, tonight, tomorrow*.
- **Avoid *dependable*, *near by*, *have got*, and *fix* (to mean "repair").** All are standard now.
- **No *however* at the start of a sentence.** Allowed today; just don't overuse it.
- **Strunk's preferences on *data* (always plural), *claim*, *state*, and *possess*:** follow the
  house style or context rather than forcing them.

## Quick checklist

- [ ] Every paragraph has one topic and opens with it
- [ ] Active voice wherever the actor matters
- [ ] No *not*-hedging; positive, definite claims
- [ ] Concrete specifics instead of abstractions
- [ ] No *the fact that*, *there is/are … that*, *in order to*, *very*, *really*, *quite* padding
- [ ] Parallel lists and correlatives
- [ ] Modifiers next to what they modify; no dangling openers
- [ ] Commas: serial, paired around parentheticals, none splicing clauses
- [ ] The most important words land at the end
